rowscale: instrumenting row_last_copy's magnitude
==================================================

Context: takeworth.rs (cardworth clone, FINDINGS.txt) established that a
chosen Take's mean total score delta is +384.76, of which row_last_copy
alone contributes +374.13 (~97%), while the weight on that key is small and
NEGATIVE (frozen 2026-08-06 champion -4.17, live champion -3.16). This file
(rust/src/bin/rowscale.rs, new binary, 3 unit tests, all passing) explains
WHERE that magnitude comes from: is row_last_copy a sum over many row slots,
a division blowing up near zero, an unbounded accumulation, or something
else -- and whether the ~6x cost-1-vs-cost-3 scaling reported earlier is a
scarcity effect or a row-position effect.

Weights used: /private/tmp/rowdig/frozen_champion_2p.json, as instructed.
NOTE: this file's row_last_copy weight is -3.164818673692828 -- it matches
the value the task brief calls "the current live one (-3.16)", not the
"frozen 2026-08-06 champion (-4.17)" that cardworth's own
frozen_champion_2p.json holds (md5-different, verified: -4.166050796498309).
Used the file exactly as instructed regardless; all numbers below are
self-consistent under -3.1648 and are NOT directly comparable in absolute
score-point terms to takeworth_200.txt's -4.17-weighted numbers, though the
qualitative conclusions (below) hold under both.

Sample: 200 self-play 2p games, seed 1..200, both seats playing this
weights file. 400 player-games, 4952 Take decisions (fewer than cardworth's
7031 because a different, less-negative row_last_copy weight changes which
moves the bot actually chooses -- not a bug, a different policy).

-------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. What row_last_copy computes (rust/src/bots/weighted/row.rs:435-495)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
Doc comment, quoted in full because it states the author's intent directly:

  "Value sitting in the row that, if I pass it up, I will never see again.
  THE FEATURE THE AUDIT WAS ACTUALLY ASKED FOR, in the project owner's own
  words: 'know that a second selective breeding is near the end or not and
  it has to build another farm since it won't see it'. row_pressure already
  knows a card is about to slide off the LEFT; it has never known whether
  another copy is coming off the deck, so a last copy and a card with three
  more behind it price identically.

  Each takeable slot contributes card_potential * P(no further copy), and
  that probability is max(0, 1 - outlook) where outlook is
  counting::civil_outlook's expected number of further copies. The clip is
  the whole model and it has no fitted constant in it: at an outlook of 0
  the deck provably holds no more ... so the probability is exactly 1; at
  an outlook of 1 or more another copy is expected, so it is 0; in between
  it interpolates."

Plain terms: for every row slot the acting player could legally take right
now, multiply "how much is this card worth to me" (card_potential) by "how
sure am I this is my last chance at this specific card" (gone, in [0,1]),
and sum across the row. It is meant to catch a case the older row_pressure
feature (row_urgency/row_bargain_forgone) structurally cannot see: sliding
off the left edge of the row is visible to row_pressure, but a card
vanishing because the DECK ran out of copies (regardless of row position)
is not, so row_last_copy exists specifically to add that signal.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Instrumentation: where the magnitude comes from
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
Binary: rust/src/bin/rowscale.rs. For every chosen Take it replicates
row_last_copy's own per-slot loop (pinned equal to row::row_last_copy to
float precision by test full_row_last_copy_matches_row_rs) and reports, in
addition to the full before/after/delta already known from takeworth:
  - val = card_potential(taken card), gone = P(no further copy), separately
  - contributing_slots_before: how many OTHER row slots also have a
    positive term in the same sum
  - other_slots_sum_before: the sum of every slot EXCEPT the taken one

Run: 200 games, frozen weights, n=4952 Take samples (rowscale_200.txt).

  ALL takes (n=4952):
    full_before        min=0.000  median=9.407   mean=31.125  max=705.226
    full_after         min=0.000  median=0.217   mean=2.478   max=140.066
    full_delta          mean=-28.647  (min=-679.763, max=0.000)
    weighted contribution (rlc * delta), mean = +90.66

    taken card's own factors:
      val (card_potential)   median=4.703   mean=24.840   max=678.256
      gone (P no further copy) median=1.000  mean=0.669   max=1.000
      own_term = val*gone     median=4.703   mean=24.811   max=678.256

    everything else in the row-sum:
      other_slots_sum_before  median=0.382   mean=6.314   max=197.826
      contributing_slots_before (count) median=3  mean=2.830  max=9

    taken card's own_term / full_before, ratio of means = 0.797

MECHANISM: NOT "a sum over many row slots." The row only ever has ~2.8
qualifying slots on average (ROW_SIZE=6 total, most slots gated out by
can_take_gated / val<=0 / gone<=0), and the taken card's OWN term is ~80%
of the full row-sum on average (rising to 90% at cost 3, see below) -- the
"everything else" slice (other_slots_sum_before, mean 6.3) is small next to
the taken card's own term (mean 24.8). This matches takeworth's earlier,
independent finding (94-98.5% of the raw DELTA attributable to the taken
card's own before-state term at cost 1/2/3).

NOT "a division by something near zero" either: gone is a clean [0,1]
clip with "no fitted constant in it" per the doc comment above, and it sits
at a median of 1.000 and a mean of 0.669 -- nowhere near an unstable
denominator (there is no division in the formula at all; gone is a
subtraction-then-clamp, val*gone is a product).

The actual mechanism is (c): card_potential itself is a large, effectively
UNBOUNDED number, not a bounded feature. card_potential (cards.rs:1639) is
"priced through w": it recurses into tech_value/gov_value/action_value,
each of which does its own w.get(SomeKey) * amount and sums a card's whole
printed yield line (culture, science, production, workers, etc.) through
the SAME weight vector evaluate() itself uses (CardYield's own doc comment:
"the same weight-vector namespace evaluate itself indexes"). So a single
call to card_potential is not a raw count like "civil_actions remaining" or
"discontent" (the kind of quantity every OTHER WeightKey in weights.rs
multiplies, all naturally bounded to small integers) -- it is already a
composite, ALREADY-WEIGHTED sub-evaluation, capable of reaching the
hundreds on its own (measured max here: 678.256, on ONE card). row_last_copy
then multiplies that already-large number by a SECOND weight (rlc, -3.16),
compounding an eval-scale quantity through another weight multiplication --
the source of the size mismatch against every other WeightKey's
contribution (which multiply a weight against a small bounded count).

-------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Is it SUPPOSED to be this dominant?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
The doc comment (quoted in full above) states an INTENT -- catch scarcity
row_pressure cannot see -- and separately, carefully, defends the SHAPE of
the gone/outlook clip ("the clip is the whole model and it has no fitted
constant in it"). It says nothing at all about the intended MAGNITUDE or
about calibrating card_potential's own scale before folding it into this
sum. There is no comment anywhere in row.rs or weights.rs asserting
row_last_copy should dominate ~80-97% of a Take's total score, or that it
should be allowed to reach the hundreds on a single card while every other
WeightKey contribution in the same evaluate() call stays in the
single/low-double digits (see takeworth's own top-12 list: ca_left +12.32,
hand_value_early -9.42, row_urgency +7.16, everything else < 3). The
observed dominance is real and reproducible, but it is not something the
author's own comments claim was the design goal -- the doc comment defends
correctness of the probability clip, not the resulting scale.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. Why does it scale with cost (cheap vs expensive slot)?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
Same run, split by take cost:

  cost=1 (n=1488): val mean=8.592   gone mean=0.521  own_term mean=8.547
  cost=2 (n=1343): val mean=18.970  gone mean=0.623  own_term mean=18.938
  cost=3 (n=2100): val mean=40.351  gone mean=0.809  own_term mean=40.335

  cost3/cost1 ratio:  val = 4.70x   gone = 1.55x   own_term = 4.72x

val (card_potential -- "how good is this card") grows ~4.7x from cost-1 to
cost-3 takes. gone (P(no further copy) -- the scarcity signal) grows only
~1.55x over the same span. own_term's growth (4.72x) tracks val's growth
almost exactly, not gone's. (Earlier cardworth work, under the -4.17
weight and a larger/different sample, reported a steeper ~6x scaling of
the full row_last_copy contribution 87->533; the same decomposition logic
applies there -- val dominates the ratio, gone is the minor factor -- this
run's absolute numbers differ because the weight and bot policy differ,
but the ATTRIBUTION direction is the same.)

Also: cost=1's val MEDIAN is 0.001 (essentially zero) vs cost=3's median of
15.226 -- more than half of cost-1 takes have a taken card whose own
card_potential term is gated to ~nothing (redundant / already covered /
val<=0), while cost-3 takes (freshly dealt cards, the expensive end of the
row) are reliably worth something.

CONCLUSION: the cost-1-to-cost-3 scaling is dominantly a ROW-POSITION /
card-quality effect, not a scarcity effect. Cards enter the row at the
expensive (cost-3) end and slide toward cost-1 as the sweep runs; a cost-1
card has typically already been passed over by every player once (its own
card_potential often already near zero -- not worth taking, which is
consistent with it having survived to the cheap end unclaimed), while a
cost-3 card is untested and, on average, simply a much bigger number. gone
does rise somewhat with cost (0.52 -> 0.81) -- freshly dealt cards do skew
toward being scarcer on average too -- but that factor is a much smaller
contributor (1.55x) than val's (4.70x) to the overall ~4.7x scaling.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. Verdict: is there a defect, and what shape is it?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
Not a wrong-sign bug: the negative weight, applied to a feature that is
evaluated on the POST-move state (same convention as every other feature),
mechanically rewards Take over EndTurn because taking a card visibly
removes that card's own term from the row-sum in the after-state (which
EndTurn's after-state does not) -- multiplying a lower after-sum by a
negative weight raises the score. That is a coherent, intentional way to
encode "minimize scarce, unclaimed value left in the row," matching the
doc comment's stated intent. Not a division-by-near-zero bug: there is no
division in the formula, and the one clipped ratio (gone) sits centered
near its bounds, not near an unstable edge.

The defect, if there is one, is in NORMALIZATION / SCALE, not in the
formula's logic: row_last_copy sums card_potential -- an already fully
weight-priced, unbounded eval-scale quantity (same namespace and same
weight vector evaluate() itself uses, capable of reaching several hundred
points on a single card, measured max 678.256 here) -- and then multiplies
that sum by a SECOND weight. Every other WeightKey in weights.rs prices a
small, naturally bounded raw count (workers, civil actions, discontent,
stock levels) exactly once. row_last_copy is structurally different: it is
one weighted composite folded into another, which is why its swings run
1-2 orders of magnitude larger than every other term in the same evaluate()
call (takeworth's top-12: everything else in the list is single/low-double
digits; row_last_copy alone is +374 of a +384 total). Whether that
magnitude is a problem worth changing is exactly the question this task
was told NOT to answer -- no weights, no formulas, no clamps were touched
here. What is established, with numbers: the mechanism is "one already-
large per-card value, multiplied by a second weight, dominated by the
taken card's own term (~80-90% of the row-sum)" -- not a sum-over-many-
slots effect, not a near-zero-division blowup, and its cost-1-to-cost-3
growth is chiefly a row-position/card-quality effect (val, ~4.7x), with
scarcity (gone, ~1.55x) playing a distinctly secondary role.
