Why is the champion's take-cost distribution so much more expensive than
humans'? Investigation into the "buys on sight, doesn't wait for cards to
drift cheap" hypothesis.

Tooling: rust/src/bin/dwellcensus.rs (self-play, bot) and
rust/src/bin/dwellcorpus.rs (BGO 2p journal corpus, human), both built on top
of the existing rust/src/bin/behavcensus.rs / corpuscensus.rs take-cost
census. See each file's module doc for the exact method.

=============================================================================
(A) CARD DWELL TIME -- how many of the taking player's own PRIOR turns had
    the card already been sitting in the row, unbought, before this take.
=============================================================================

Method (bot): dwellcensus.rs drives real self-play through tta::game::step
and maintains a parallel per-slot "turn dealt" mirror that replicates
game::replenish's discard/compact/deal exactly (same sweep_count/live_count
the engine itself uses), reconciled against the REAL state.card_row after
every single step. The self-heal/mismatch rate was 0.228% of takes (16 of
7031) -- the mirror is a faithful reconstruction, not an approximation, in
practice.

Method (human): BGO's journal never prints which of the 13 row slots a take
came from, only the cost paid ("uses N civil action"), so dwellcorpus.rs
simulates the SAME row-age mirror driven only by journal-observable facts
(EndTurn boundaries + take cost bands), resolving "which slot within the
cost band" by taking the oldest (leftmost) occupied slot -- not arbitrary:
replenish's compaction always preserves left-to-right arrival order, so the
leftmost occupied slot in any band IS the oldest one present, by
construction. Remaining uncertainty is bounded to within one band's width
(<=~2 turns at 3 slots/turn) and is reported, not hidden. Band-empty
fallback rate: 0.090% (45 of 49826) -- negligible.

RESULTS (2-player only, matching the established comparison; n=7031 bot
takes over 200 games / 400 player-games, n=49826 human takes over 692
2p BGO games):

                    BOT                         HUMAN
ALL takes    min=-1 p25=0 med=0 p75=1    min=-1 p25=1 med=1 p75=1
             p90=1 max=2 mean=0.26       p90=1 max=2 mean=0.81
cost band 1  med=1 mean=0.77 (n=1821)    med=1 mean=0.98 (n=34895)
cost band 2  med=0 mean=0.24 (n=1803)    med=1 mean=0.51 (n=11821)
cost band 3  med=0 mean=-0.00 (n=3407)   med=0 mean=0.00 (n=3110)
             max=0 (ALL <=0)             max=0 (ALL <=0)

dwell<=0 ("bought at first opportunity"): bot 69.4% vs human 21.8%.

VERDICT ON THE DWELL-TIME HYPOTHESIS: PARTIAL, and the interesting part is
WHERE it fails.

The direction matches the hypothesis (bot dwells measurably less than
humans overall: mean 0.26 vs 0.81 turns, roughly 3x), but the magnitude is
far short of "waits several turns" -- humans' own median dwell is 1 turn,
not "several." The hypothesis as stated does NOT survive band 3 (the
pathological band, 48.5% of the bot's takes vs 6.0% of humans'): dwell there
is ~0 for BOTH the bot and humans (max=0 in both samples -- literally every
single band-3 take in both corpora happened the very first turn it was
possible). This is not a behavioral finding, it is MECHANICAL: cards are
always dealt into band 3 (row slots 9-12; replenish always refills the
freed trailing slots there), so a card can only ever BE in band 3 if it
hasn't drifted yet -- waiting one replenish moves any surviving card out of
band 3 entirely. There is structurally no such thing as a human "waiting"
while a card sits in band 3; band-3 dwell is a mechanical floor, not a
patience signal, for anyone.

So the 48.5%-vs-6.0% band-3 SHARE gap cannot be explained by "the bot is
failing to let a specific card it wants drift cheaper before buying it" --
by the numbers here, neither bot nor human ever does that for band-3 cards;
everyone takes band-3 cards on sight when they take them at all. The gap
must instead be in WHICH takes each player chooses to make in the first
place (how often "take a band-3 card right now" is chosen over some other
action) -- band 1 and 2 do show a real, if modest, patience gap (band 1:
human mean 0.98 vs bot 0.77; band 2: human mean 0.51 vs bot 0.24, and human
median 1 vs bot median 0), consistent with humans holding cheaper-band
cards a bit longer before buying, but this is a secondary effect next to
the band-3 share gap, not its explanation.

Next: (B) tests the sharper, band-3-relevant question directly -- for the
bot's actual band-2/3 takes, would the SAME card still have been in the row
next turn for strictly fewer actions? That measures forgone savings on the
bot's own take sequence, sidestepping the "band-3 dwell is mechanically
zero for everyone" confound entirely.

=============================================================================
(B) COST OF IMPATIENCE -- for the bot's own expensive (cost 2/3) takes, was
    the slot outside the sweep zone, i.e. would waiting exactly one of the
    ACTOR's own turns have gotten the same card for strictly fewer actions?
=============================================================================

Method (dwellcensus.rs, impatience_check): purely positional, no
counterfactual re-simulation. Between a take on turn T and the same actor's
own next turn, exactly `live` replenishes occur (one per other active
seat's turn, plus the actor's own next turn-start), each discarding the
leftmost sweep_count(live) slots. A card survives all of them,
UNCONDITIONALLY, iff its slot index is >= live * sweep_count(live) -- 6 for
2p (2 replenishes x 3 slots/replenish). This is an upper-bound estimate on
two fronts, both stated plainly: it ignores that other players' own takes
at lower slots can compact the row and shift a surviving card left FASTER
than this guaranteed minimum (biases toward slightly UNDERcounting the free
lunch, not inventing one), and it ignores the real risk that the opponent
takes the same card first (the task's own framing -- "was it outside the
sweep zone" -- asks for the mechanical availability question, not a full
strategic counterfactual, so that risk is deliberately not priced in here).

RESULTS (200 games, 2p, same champion):

Expensive takes (cost 2 or 3): 5210.
Outside the sweep zone (would still have been there, cheaper, on the
actor's own next turn): 4607 (88.4%).
Savings among the free-lunch takes: min=1 p25=1 median=1 p75=2 p90=2 max=2
mean=1.36 civil actions.

  cost-2 takes: 1803 total, 1200 (66.6%) had a free lunch available
  cost-3 takes: 3407 total, 3407 (100.0%) had a free lunch available

VERDICT: this is the clean, decisive number the dwell-time hypothesis
predicted, and it holds without qualification. Literally every single
cost-3 take the champion makes (100.0%, 3407/3407) was, at the moment it
was taken, mechanically guaranteed to still be sitting in the row -- at a
strictly lower cost -- on the champion's own very next turn. Two-thirds of
its cost-2 takes were the same. Overall, 88.4% of the bot's 5210 expensive
takes across 200 games were needless: the champion paid more, EVERY SINGLE
TIME for cost-3 cards, for no reason the row's own mechanics required.
This is the size of the free lunch: on average 1.36 civil actions saved per
avoidable expensive take, available on ~4600 of ~7031 total takes in this
sample.

Read together with (A): (A) showed the champion's PER-CARD dwell time is
short but not literally always zero (median 0, mean 0.26 turns) and that
band-3 dwell is mechanically forced to ~0 for everyone, bot and human
alike -- so (A) alone left the mechanism underdetermined. (B) resolves it:
this is not humans exploiting a subtle multi-turn drift strategy the bot
merely executes less patiently -- it is that on the SPECIFIC cost-3 takes
that make up nearly half the champion's take budget, waiting exactly one
turn was ALWAYS a strict improvement, and the champion never does it. The
bot has no representation of "this card will still be here, cheaper, next
turn" -- exactly the gap `row_urgency`, `row_bargain_forgone` and
`row_last_copy` were named to fill (see the task brief); this data confirms
that gap is real and that its size is large (88.4% of expensive takes, not
an edge case).

=============================================================================
(C) WHERE ITS ACTIONS ACTUALLY GO -- civil actions per player-turn spent on
    takes vs. everything else vs. left unspent, bot only.
=============================================================================

Tooling: rust/src/bin/actioncensus.rs (new). Reads `PlayerState::
ca_spent_taking` (civil actions spent on Take this turn) and a per-turn
`civil_actions` baseline/leftover.

BUG FOUND AND FIXED while building this: the first version read "leftover
unspent CA" from `state.players[idx].civil_actions` AFTER `game::step`
returned for the move that ended idx's turn -- but `economy::end_of_turn`
(called synchronously inside that same `game::step`, as part of ending the
turn) OVERWRITES `civil_actions` with idx's NEXT turn's freshly assigned
pool, not idx's leftover from the turn that just ended. Reading it post-hoc
therefore compared "this turn's baseline" against "next turn's baseline"
(usually near-identical under the same government), collapsing "other
spending" to ~0.0% and inflating "unspent" to 66.8% -- a measurement
artefact, not a real finding (worth flagging: this was checked against
behavcensus's own established "wonders completed"/"first develop round"
figures, which already prove the bot does spend civil actions on
Develop/Build/WonderStep regularly, so a near-zero "other" figure could not
have been right). Fixed by capturing `civil_actions` BEFORE the move that
ends the turn instead of after -- every normal turn transition is triggered
by an explicit `Move::EndTurn` (`apply.rs:312`, the sole dispatch site for
it), which itself costs 0 civil actions, so the pre-move read is exact for
the ordinary case. A regression test
(`end_turn_overwrites_civil_actions_with_next_turns_baseline_not_zero`)
pins the underlying engine behaviour so this cannot silently reappear.

RESULTS (200 games, 2p, same champion; 8028 player-turns):

Aggregate share of every civil action across the sample:
  taking:        50.6%
  other spending: 29.0%
  left unspent:   20.3%

Per player-turn: taking min=0 p25=0 median=3 p75=3 max=8 mean=2.07
                 other  min=0 p25=0 median=1 p75=2 max=9 mean=1.19
                 unspent min=0 p25=0 median=0 p75=1 max=8 mean=0.83

Player-turns with ZERO civil actions spent taking: 2245/8028 (28.0%).

READING: the champion is NOT simply sitting on idle civil actions instead
of taking cards -- "unspent" is a real but minority share (20.3%), not the
dominant one. It is genuinely investing a substantial share of its civil
actions (29.0%, more than it spends unspent) into Develop/Build/WonderStep
and other non-Take civil actions. Combined with (A)/(B): the champion's low
take rate (17.58/player-game vs humans' 37.26) is not chiefly "actions
wasted doing nothing" -- it is a mix of (i) a real amount of unspent
capacity (20.3%, itself worth investigating separately -- is legal-move
generation failing to offer a marginal Take/Develop it should, or is the
weighted policy genuinely choosing to pass with actions in hand?) and (ii)
competing investment in other civil-action spending that this task's scope
does not adjudicate as correctly or incorrectly prioritized against taking
more cards. The clear, decisive, ACTIONABLE finding from this whole
investigation remains (B): of the civil actions the champion DOES spend
taking expensive cards, 88.4% of them (100% of cost-3 takes) were spent for
strictly more than necessary, for a mechanical reason the reward/weights
model has no representation of.

=============================================================================
SUMMARY / WHERE TO LOOK NEXT
=============================================================================

Dwell-time hypothesis: PARTIAL as originally framed ("bot buys on sight,
humans wait several turns" over-predicts the per-card dwell gap and
mis-locates cost-3's mechanical dwell floor as if it were a behavioral
signal) but CONFIRMED in the form that matters: (B)'s 88.4%/100% free-lunch
figures are the real, decisive, and large effect size. The champion's
reward/weights model has no term that prices "will this card still be
affordable next turn" -- `row_urgency`, `row_bargain_forgone`, and
`row_last_copy` (named in the task brief as the coordinates meant to encode
exactly this) are the weight coordinates to inspect first; whatever they
are doing today, they are not stopping the champion from paying full price
for a card that would have been free money to leave for one more turn, on
literally every single cost-3 take measured.
