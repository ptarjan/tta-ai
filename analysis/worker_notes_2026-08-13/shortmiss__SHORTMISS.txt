0. HEADLINE ANSWER (MEASURED -- see sections 4-7 for full detail)
===========================================================================
CONCENTRATED, not random noise -- but not on the specific categories the
task's own hypothesis named. MISS rate overall is 4.88% (1,087/22,287
non-trivial holdout decisions; reproduces the documented 95.12% top-8 hit
rate exactly). The MISS RATE itself is fairly evenly spread once you
control for legal-move count (structurally 0% when <=8 legal moves exist at
all, 12-14% whenever there are >10). But the COST of a miss is savagely
concentrated: the worst 1% of misses (11 of 1,087 decisions) account for
32% of ALL lost search-eval value in the entire 22,287-decision holdout;
the worst 10% (109 decisions) account for 71%. One rare category --
colonization-auction bidding ("Bid", 566 decisions, only 19 misses, a
BELOW-average 3.36% miss rate) -- averages 187.9 eval points of cost per
miss when it does miss, ~17x every other category, and by itself accounts
for 29.5% of the ENTIRE dataset's lost value from 1.7% of its misses.
CONTRARY to the task's a priori worry, the two categories it explicitly
named as high-stakes -- aggression_or_war (declaring/fighting a war) and
leader_or_wonder_step (taking a wonder or a leader) -- are UNDER, not over,
the base rate: 0/410 misses and 6/566 (1.06%) misses respectively, both
cheap when they do miss. The real volume driver is take_card (17.0% miss
rate, 476 of the 1,087 misses, 28% of total cost) -- a routine, frequent
decision, not a rare one. So: the 95.12% headline number understates real
risk, but the mechanism is a heavy-tailed COST distribution concentrated in
one rare, expensive category (colonization bidding) plus a high-volume,
moderately-expensive routine category (take_card) -- not the war/wonder/
leader decisions the task suspected going in.

===========================================================================
3. METHOD FOR THE COST/STRUCTURAL ANALYSIS
===========================================================================
The `.tpd`/`.meta.tsv` pair the command above writes records WHICH move the
search chose and WHERE it ranked under the policy -- enough to reproduce the
95.12%-style top-8 hit rate, but NOT what the search's own evaluation
thought the alternatives were worth, which is what "cost" (section 5) needs.

To get that without re-running a second, differently-behaved search, a new
binary was added: rust/src/bin/shortmiss_scan.rs. It reuses
`policyloop::build_shortlist` UNCHANGED (identical exploration shortlist,
identical RNG consumption to `policyloop_gen`) but calls `plan::rank`
instead of `plan::pick`. `plan::rank`'s own doc comment guarantees its
winner (`.first()` after a stable descending sort) is IDENTICAL to `pick`'s
own choice -- same `search_totals` call, same tie-break -- so this is not a
second search with different behaviour, it is the same search with the
losing candidates' scores kept instead of thrown away. This is what lets
"cost of a miss" be measured: for every decision, the best score among the
plain top-8 shortlist candidates (by policy rank) versus the score of
whatever the search actually chose.

Pinned config, matching the official regeneration exactly: 100 games, 2p,
seeds 100001..100100, shortlist_width=8, explore_random=4, champion
/private/tmp/rowdig/frozen_champion_2p.json, human weights
../analysis/frozen/human_weights.json, plan defaults (width=8,
max_nodes=4000, matching PlanConfig::default()).

Command:
  cd /private/tmp/shortmiss/rust && cargo run --profile difftest --bin shortmiss_scan

HIT/MISS definition: HIT = chosen_policy_rank < 8 (the search's actual pick
sat inside the policy's plain top-8, i.e. what a non-exploring
`root_shortlist` would also have offered). MISS = chosen_policy_rank >= 8.
This matches POLICYTRAIN1.txt section 4's own "top-8 (shortlist-hit)"
definition exactly.

Category: `human_policy::categorize(chosen_move)` -- the project's existing
exhaustive `match` over the real `Move` enum (no wildcard arm; a new `Move`
variant is a compile error there, not a silent `Other`). Not a hand-written
string list.

Seat position: `game::scores(state)` (the public culture track) at decision
time, acting seat vs the best-placed rival -- ahead / behind / tied. Public
information only (culture tokens are face-up on the board); no rival hand
contents or deck order read anywhere on this path.

Cost: for a MISS, `search's own score for the chosen move` minus `the best
search score among shortlist candidates whose policy rank is < 8` (both
read off the SAME `plan::rank` call, no extra search). Clamped at 0 (a
negative "cost" would mean the search-preferred top-8 alternative scored
higher than what the search actually picked, which cannot happen by
construction: the chosen move is the argmax over the WHOLE widened
shortlist, a superset of the top-8). HITs cost exactly 0 by the same
construction (the chosen move IS the argmax over the top-8 in that case).
"Unscored" misses (score missing for the chosen move, or for every top-8
candidate, because the beam's `cfg.max_nodes` budget starved before a
terminal value was ever recorded for one side) are counted separately, not
folded into the distribution as 0.

===========================================================================
7. HYPOTHESISED (interpretation -- clearly separated from the MEASURED
   numbers above; nothing in this section is a new measurement)
===========================================================================
- The task's a priori worry -- that misses clump on rare, high-stakes
  categories like declaring war or taking a wonder/leader -- does NOT hold
  for THOSE specific categories in this holdout (aggression_or_war: 0
  misses; leader_or_wonder_step: 1.06% miss rate, cheap when it misses).
  A plausible reason (not verified further here): `human_policy`'s linear
  ranker may simply be well-suited to these decisions because they are
  comparatively rare AND highly differentiated by a few strong features
  (e.g. "is this wonder good", "is my military strong enough") that a
  linear scorer can weight cleanly, whereas...
- ...colonization-auction bidding ("Bid") fits the ORIGINAL worry's SHAPE
  even though it wasn't named: rare (566/22,287 = 2.5% of decisions), a
  narrow decision (bid amount `n: u8`, a small ordered set of legal
  amounts), and its miss rate is unremarkable (3.36%, actually below the
  4.88% base rate) -- but when it misses, it is catastrophically expensive
  (187.9 eval points average, the highest of any category by a wide
  margin, and roughly 40x take_card's own per-miss cost). This looks like
  exactly the failure mode the task was worried about, just filed under a
  different category name than the task guessed: an auction is a genuinely
  discontinuous, all-or-nothing decision (win the colony or don't), so a
  policy that ranks the WRONG bid into the top-8 can be leaving an entire
  colony's value on the table, not a marginal card-choice difference.
- take_card is the largest single contributor to total cost (28% of it)
  purely on VOLUME (476 misses, the most of any category) at a moderate
  per-miss cost (7.2) -- this reads as "the policy is measurably worse at
  ranking which market card to take than at ranking most other decision
  types" (17.02% miss rate, more than 3x the base rate), not as a rare/
  high-stakes problem; it is a routine, extremely frequent decision.
- The legal-move-count finding (section 4) suggests part of the "5% miss"
  headline is a fixed-width artifact, not a policy-quality one: a
  shortlist of width 8 cannot possibly miss when 8 or fewer moves are
  legal, so the entire miss rate is necessarily concentrated in decisions
  with >8 alternatives (46% of this holdout's non-trivial decisions), where
  it runs 12-14%, not 4.88%. If the task's real question is "how good is
  the shortlist when it actually has to choose", 12-14% (not 4.88%) is the
  more honest headline for the decisions where the shortlist's width is
  actually being tested.
- Given the KNOWN LIMITATION (old champion), these cost NUMBERS would
  likely shift under the current, corrected evaluator -- but the
  STRUCTURAL finding (cost is tail-concentrated; branching factor and the
  Bid category drive it; the named "high-stakes" categories are actually
  fine) depends on the shortlist-construction mechanism and the shape of
  the decision space, not on the specific eval weights, and is not expected
  to change qualitatively on a re-run with the fixed champion.
